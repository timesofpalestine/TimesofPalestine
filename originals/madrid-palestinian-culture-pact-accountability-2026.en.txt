Twenty-eight governments and the Arab League Educational, Cultural and Scientific Organization have signed a declaration calling for an international pact to protect Palestinian culture. The document establishes principles and an ambition. It does not yet establish the machinery that would turn either into a recovery programme.

That distinction is visible in the signed text itself. The [two-page declaration adopted in Madrid on 16 July](https://www.cultura.gob.es/dam/jcr%3Af57d3f1f-beb4-40bf-96ca-3fb2f85d6415/conference-palestinian-culture----final-declaration.pdf) calls on states, international organizations, financial and philanthropic institutions, cultural bodies and civil society to work toward an International Pact for the Resilience of Palestinian Culture. It says the pact should mobilize political commitment, expertise and resources.

It does not specify how much money should be raised, who will hold or distribute it, which Palestinian body will set priorities, when the pact will begin operating, or how the public will be able to measure delivery.

Those omissions do not make the declaration meaningless. Thirty delegations met, and signatories spanning Europe, the Arab world, Asia and Latin America agreed that culture belongs inside humanitarian response, reconstruction and peacebuilding. But the scale of Palestinian cultural loss is already quantified in ways that make the next administrative decisions urgent. A 2026 assessment led by the World Bank, European Union and United Nations estimates $230 million in cultural-heritage recovery and reconstruction needs in Gaza and the West Bank.

Madrid produced political agreement. The next test is whether that agreement becomes a Palestinian-led, funded and publicly accountable system.

## Key findings

- **The declaration is a framework, not an operating fund.** It states three principles and calls for a resilience pact, but names no budget, secretariat, governing board, implementation calendar or measurable target.
- **The signatory list is broad but not universal.** Twenty-eight governments, including Palestine, and ALECSO endorsed the text. The official account says 30 international delegations attended.
- **The documented need is much larger than existing disclosed cultural programmes.** The 2026 rapid damage and needs assessment estimates $150 million in physical cultural-heritage damage, $90 million in economic losses and $230 million in recovery needs.
- **Different public totals measure different things.** UNESCO’s $5.7 million emergency package covers culture, education and media together; the $230 million assessment figure concerns cultural heritage. They should not be treated as directly comparable budget lines.
- **The accountability questions are now concrete.** Who decides, who pays, who administers, which Palestinian institutions lead, what gets protected first, and what evidence will show that the pact has moved beyond a declaration?

## What the declaration actually commits to

The declaration begins from three principles. Culture should be treated as a strategic part of humanitarian response, recovery, reconstruction and peacebuilding. Protection should extend beyond monuments to institutions, cultural workers and diverse forms of expression. Cultural reconstruction should help restore identity, resilience and the foundations of peace.

It then calls for the international pact. The proposed vehicle is meant to protect and revive cultural life, strengthen institutions, heritage and creators, and bring together political support, expertise and money.

This is a genuine diplomatic step. The [Spanish government’s account of the conference](https://www.lamoncloa.gob.es/lang/en/gobierno/news/Paginas/2026/20260722-palestinian-culture-conference.aspx) lists Austria, Algeria, Bahrain, Belgium, the United Arab Emirates, Egypt, Spain, France, Greece, Indonesia, Ireland, Iceland, Italy, Jordan, Kuwait, Libya, Luxembourg, Mauritania, Mexico, Pakistan, Palestine, Portugal, Somalia, Syria, Tunisia, Turkey, Uruguay and Vietnam as signatory states. ALECSO also signed.

The geography matters. The list joins governments with different diplomatic positions, cultural systems and donor capacities around a common statement. It also gives the proposed pact a base wider than one European ministry or one United Nations agency.

But signing the declaration did not bind each government to a stated financial contribution. The document contains no contribution table, minimum pledge or formula for sharing the cost. It does not identify a lead institution or say whether the pact will become a legal entity, a donor coordination platform, a UNESCO programme, or a network administered by the Palestinian Culture Ministry.

The declaration is therefore best read as authorization to build a mechanism, not proof that the mechanism exists.

## The scale behind the diplomatic language

The latest system-wide estimate makes the missing operational detail more consequential.

The [April 2026 rapid damage and needs assessment](https://thedocs.worldbank.org/en/doc/e539cbf23b348c3d4fc69b8a7e9c9d7d-0280062026/original/Gaza-RDNA-2026-Final-April-19.pdf), prepared through the World Bank, European Union and United Nations assessment process, assigns the cultural-heritage sector $150 million in physical damage, $90 million in economic losses and $230 million in recovery and reconstruction needs.

These categories are not interchangeable. Damage is the estimated value of destroyed or impaired physical assets. Losses describe interrupted economic flows and activity. Needs estimate the financing required for recovery and reconstruction. Adding damage and losses does not produce the needs total, because each measure answers a different question.

The assessment is also broader than a list of famous monuments. Cultural recovery includes the systems around physical heritage: archives, collections, professional skills, documentation, conservation capacity and the institutions that make heritage accessible.

UNESCO’s [site-by-site damage register](https://www.unesco.org/en/gaza/assessment) provides another view. As of 24 March, the agency had verified damage to 164 cultural sites since 7 October 2023: 14 religious sites, 128 buildings of historical or artistic interest, three repositories of movable cultural property, nine monuments, two museums and eight archaeological sites.

UNESCO describes that count as a preliminary assessment. It uses satellite imagery and analysis supplied by UNITAR-UNOSAT and, where access and security allow, rapid on-site assessments. The list is evidence of verified damage, not a complete census of every cultural loss.

Palestinian administrative data reach beyond buildings. In a [National Culture Day release](https://www.pcbs.gov.ps/en/post-details/?postId=22353), the Palestinian Central Bureau of Statistics and Culture Ministry reported that 118 cultural-sector workers in Gaza were killed during 2024. They said all 25 culture centres registered with the ministry suffered complete or partial destruction, all 87 public and institutional libraries were damaged, and eight of 15 publishing houses and printing presses were completely destroyed.

Those Palestinian figures and UNESCO’s register should not be merged into one total. They use different definitions, methods and reporting periods. Read together, they show why a pact limited to repairing stone would be incomplete. Cultural recovery also concerns people, livelihoods, collections, performances, publishing, memory and the ability to create.

## Existing money is not the new pact

There is already international spending connected to culture and Gaza, but the public figures do not amount to a financing plan for the Madrid pact.

UNESCO reported in April that it had mobilized [$5.7 million in emergency assistance](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/unesco-delivers-recovery-efforts-gaza-57-million-emergency-assistance-and-calls-continued) and was seeking $116.5 million. That package spans three fields: cultural heritage, education and support for journalists and media. UNESCO said five heritage sites had been stabilized, seven more were undergoing work, and the programme had generated 1,850 workdays for 30 local workers.

These are specific outputs. They demonstrate that cultural protection can create both conservation work and livelihoods. They do not reveal what portion of the $5.7 million was spent on culture rather than education or media, and the $116.5 million target is not identical in scope to the assessment’s $230 million cultural-heritage needs estimate.

Spain also entered the Madrid meeting with disclosed commitments. Its government says the Culture Ministry had contributed €200,000 to a UNESCO fund for Gaza’s reconstruction and provides €100,000 a year to the TEJA emergency-support network for cultural professionals.

The conference statement presents those as existing Spanish measures. It does not say that every signatory matched them or that Madrid generated a new pooled fund. Describing the entire signatory group as having financed the pact would therefore go beyond the public record.

## Five decisions would turn a declaration into a system

The first decision is **Palestinian authority over priorities**. A resilience pact will need a published way for Palestinian public institutions, independent cultural bodies, artists, archivists, conservators and affected communities to determine what comes first. Consultation after a donor has designed the programme is not the same as shared governance from the beginning.

The second is **a financing map**. The pact needs to distinguish pledged money from deposited money and deposited money from spending. It should show restricted and unrestricted funds, administrative costs, implementing partners and the geographic distribution of grants. Otherwise a large headline pledge can coexist with local institutions that cannot pay staff or secure a damaged collection.

The third is **an accountable home**. The declaration does not name a secretariat. Whoever performs that role would need conflict-of-interest rules, procurement standards, published decisions, independent audits and a corrections process for disputed records.

The fourth is **a sequence for protection and recovery**. Emergency shoring, debris management and digitizing endangered archives cannot always wait for a complete reconstruction plan. At the same time, rebuilding without inventories, ownership records, archaeological controls and community consent can destroy evidence or replace living heritage with a generic replica.

The fifth is **measurable commitments**. A public dashboard could track sites stabilized, collections documented, cultural workers supported, institutions reopened, grants paid, archives digitized and funds spent. Targets would need to record who benefits, including women, young people, people with disabilities and cultural workers outside major cities.

None of these decisions requires reducing culture to a construction ledger. They are what allow the public to see whether money and authority reach the people whose cultural life the pact is meant to protect.

## Who is not yet inside the promise

The signatory list also defines the pact’s current diplomatic boundary. Germany, the United Kingdom, the United States, Canada, Japan, Norway and the European Union do not appear among the listed signatories, although some of those governments finance UNESCO work in Gaza or other Palestinian programmes.

Absence from this declaration does not prove refusal to support Palestinian culture. Participation in the meeting, signature of the text and funding of a separate programme are different acts. But a pact seeking durable international finance will eventually need to explain how non-signatory donors, foundations, museums, universities and diaspora institutions can join—and under whose rules.

The same applies to Palestinian cultural workers in Gaza, the West Bank, Jerusalem, Israel and the diaspora. The declaration speaks about institutions, heritage and creators as a whole. Its implementation will need to avoid treating one ministry, one territory or one donor network as the complete cultural field.

## A conference promised continuity

Madrid was the first Ministerial Conference on Palestinian Culture. Spain’s government says another country is expected to host the next edition in 2027, but the public conference account does not name that country.

An annual meeting can sustain attention. It can also become a cycle of declarations unless work continues between conferences. The strongest signal before the next gathering would not be another ceremonial endorsement. It would be a published operating design: Palestinian representation, a secretariat, a first financing round, criteria for urgent support and a timetable for independent reporting.

The Madrid declaration has done one necessary thing. It states that cultural survival is not a decorative addition to recovery. It belongs inside it.

What remains is harder and more measurable. Governments that signed now have to decide what the pact is, who controls it and what each participant will contribute. Until those decisions are published, Madrid is a promise with a destination—but without the delivery system needed to get there.
