Of everything Arab states have offered Gaza this year, one commitment is unusually concrete: a military field hospital, staffed and run by the Kingdom of Morocco, inside the Strip.

It has not been set up yet, and nobody has published a date. What follows is the record of what was promised, by whom, on which date, and where the promise now sits.

## What Morocco committed to

Foreign Minister Nasser Bourita announced the contribution at the first meeting of the Board of Peace in Washington, making Morocco the first Arab state to commit forces publicly to the International Stabilization Force.

The package he set out has four parts: troops for the force, police officers who would also train police from Gaza, senior officers for its joint military command, and the military field hospital.

Morocco signed its participating-country agreement on 15 July, placing it alongside Indonesia, Kazakhstan, Kosovo and Albania as the first five states to formalise a contribution. Uganda's parliament approved its own deployment in August, and Turkey has since offered troops.

Bourita also said Rabat would lead a programme against hate speech, described in the force's own language as deradicalisation — a framing that belongs to the force and its sponsors, not to Palestinians, who were not consulted on it.

## Where it stands

Israel's political-security cabinet approved the force's entry on 11 August, on the condition that it move in full coordination with the Israeli army, and an initial tranche was reported at around two hundred personnel from what Israeli officials called friendly countries.

Sources in Gaza reported no deployment at all through late August. No timetable for the field hospital has been published by Rabat, by the Board of Peace or by the force's command.

That is the honest state of it: signed, approved by the occupying power, unbuilt.

## Why the distinction matters here

A field hospital is not a pledge of money that can sit in a communiqué. It is a measurable object — beds, an operating theatre, surgeons, a generator, a supply line — and Gaza's health system can count whether it exists.

The health ministry in Gaza puts the medical workers killed since October 2023 at 1,580. Against that loss, a functioning Arab military hospital would be a real addition, and Morocco is the only Arab state to have committed one in writing.

The gap between the signature in July and an empty site in September is worth stating plainly, without cynicism about the intention. The commitment is genuine and specific; what Gaza needs from it now is a delivery date.
