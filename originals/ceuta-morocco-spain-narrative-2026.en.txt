A week after tens of thousands of people surged across Morocco's border into the Spanish exclave of Ceuta, a narrative is hardening on social media and in parts of the regional commentary: that Rabat engineered the crisis in coordination with Israel, to punish Madrid for mounting Europe's most forceful pro-Palestinian turn. The documented record supports a genuine rupture between Morocco and Spain, and a separate, deeper one between Spain and Israel. What no published evidence yet supports is the line connecting Rabat to Tel Aviv — and this newspaper reports that line as what it is: an allegation, not a finding.

![The crisis and the narrative: what is documented and what is not](times-of-palestine-ceuta-narrative-2026.svg)

## The crisis

In the final days of July, Spanish authorities counted roughly 50,000 crossings into Ceuta, the largest border event in the enclave's history, and at least 57 people died in the water and the crush, according to accounts carried by CNN, NBC News and Middle East Eye. Spanish officials and reporters at the fence described Moroccan border guards as largely absent while the crowds gathered — the detail that turned a migration story into a diplomatic one, because it repeated the pattern of May 2021, when Rabat relaxed its side of the fence and some 10,000 people crossed in two days after Madrid quietly hospitalized the Polisario Front's leader. The timing sharpened the reading: on June 29 Spain's Supreme Court had ruled against the summary "hot returns" Madrid long used at the Ceuta and Melilla fences, raising the legal cost of exactly the surge that followed a month later.

## Spain's Palestine turn

The theory's appeal rests on what Spain has become in Europe's Palestine politics. Pedro Sánchez's government recognized the State of Palestine, adopted the word genocide for Israel's war on Gaza, cancelled arms contracts with Israeli suppliers, and refused to open Spanish bases to the American air campaign against Iran — a record that has left relations between Madrid and Tel Aviv effectively frozen, with Israeli ministers attacking Sánchez by name. For those pushing the coordination narrative, the conclusion writes itself: Spain stood up for Palestine, and Spain's most sensitive border promptly ruptured.

## The narrative and its evidence

The claim's promoters anchor it to two real data points. In April, an opinion piece in the Israeli outlet Ynet urged Israel to support Morocco's claim to "recover" Ceuta and Melilla as payback for Madrid's positions — a published argument, but an argument by a commentator, not a policy. And Morocco and Israel have maintained normalized relations since 2020, including defense cooperation, a relationship Rabat has kept formally intact through the war even while Moroccan streets filled against it. From those two facts, viral posts have assembled a coordinated operation. Nothing published sustains the leap: no document, no official statement, and no reporting in any major outlet establishes Israeli involvement in the border surge, and Morocco needs no outside prompting for a tactic it has demonstrably used on its own — the 2021 surge was pure bilateral leverage over Western Sahara, and Western Sahara, not Gaza, remains the file on which Rabat pressures Madrid. Morocco's government had issued no response to the coordination allegation by the time of publication; its standing record on Palestine — chairing the Organization of Islamic Cooperation's Al-Quds Committee and delivering aid shipments to Gaza — is the record the allegation's promoters must explain away, and so far they have not.

## The Ramallah signal

Which is what gives Sunday's protocol its weight. Vice President Hussein al-Sheikh received Morocco's ambassador to Palestine, Abdel Rahim Meziane, in Ramallah, the official WAFA agency reported, discussing what it called the longstanding fraternal relations between the two countries; al-Sheikh asked the ambassador to convey congratulations to King Mohammed VI on the twenty-seventh anniversary of his accession, the Throne Day occasion Morocco's embassy marked with a reception in Ramallah the same week. On its face this is calendar diplomacy. Read against the week around it, it is also a message both capitals wanted sent: whatever is alleged about Rabat's hand at Ceuta, Morocco is tending its Palestinian standing in public, and Ramallah is receiving it warmly. The standard this newspaper applies to the rest is the one the graphic above draws: the rupture with Spain is documented, the Israeli hand is not, and the story changes only when evidence does.
