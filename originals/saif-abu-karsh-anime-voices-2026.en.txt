Some talents are so improbable that they rearrange your sense of what a person can do. Saif Abu Karsh is a Palestinian boy who has never seen the anime characters he loves — he is blind — and who has learned them anyway, by ear, so completely that he can summon their voices at will: the battle cries, the catchphrases, the whole vocal architecture of Japan's animated heroes, reproduced by a child who knows them as pure sound. Palestine TV told his story in a report now circulating in Arabic, and Times of Palestine features him today because his gift is its own headline — no war frame required, though he carries that too, as every Palestinian child does. Watch him perform, then try to remember that he has never seen a single frame of what he is voicing.

!video[Palestine TV's report on Saif Abu Karsh, the blind Palestinian boy who reproduces the voices of Japanese anime characters](https://youtu.be/MqNDO0FjZxs)

## An ear where the eyes should be

What Saif does belongs to a demanding craft. In Japan, the actors who voice anime — the seiyuu — train for years to build a character out of nothing but breath, pitch and timing, and the best of them become national stars. Saif works the same material from the far side of the world and without the picture: everything the animation communicates through a character's face and body has to reach him through the voice alone, and everything he gives back is voice alone. That is the marvel buried in the Palestine TV report — imitation this precise is not mimicry but analysis, a blind child's ear taking a performance apart and rebuilding it from memory. Generations of Palestinian and Arab children grew up inside dubbed anime — the afternoon cartoons that taught half the region to dream in borrowed adventures — and Saif has gone one step further than watching that world: he has moved in.

## A stage the size of his voice

Saif shares his performances on his own Facebook page, where his audience is discovering what Palestine TV's cameras found. Times of Palestine will follow him from here — this newspaper's Real Lives section exists precisely for Palestinians whose stories the news cycle would otherwise never slow down for — and we will expand this profile as his journey develops, with his family's blessing and on their terms, because he is a child and his story belongs to him first. For now, the feature is simple, and it is enough: in a place that gives its children every reason to fall silent, a blind boy has built a stage out of his own voice, and on it he can be anyone — a swordsman, a pirate, a ninja, a hero. The heroes he voices always find a way through. So, by every sign, will he.
