Ahmed Abu Amsha teaches guitar and runs a choir from a stretch of sand in central Gaza, three tents lined up within sight of the Mediterranean. Inside, the sound is a strummed guitar, a violin and a flute, the Guardian's Seham Tantesh and Jason Burke reported after visiting in July.

The tents are the Gaza branch of the Edward Said National Conservatory of Music, the Palestinian institution founded in 1993 and headquartered in the West Bank. Its Gaza office in the Tel al-Hawa neighbourhood once held three pianos, storerooms of instruments and the branch's archives.

The war destroyed all of it. Classrooms, practice rooms, the auditorium, the instruments and the archives were lost in the Israeli offensive that ran from October 2023 to October 2025, the Guardian reported.

## Teaching from what is left

Ismail Daoud, an oud player who serves as the branch's academic director, has kept lessons running for students aged 8 to 20 in tents, at Gaza College and in other improvised spaces since the destruction, according to the Institute for Palestine Studies and 972 Magazine.

Osama Jahjouh has taught flute at the conservatory since 2012. With the real ones destroyed, he now makes flutes by hand out of plastic tubing so lessons can continue, 972 Magazine reported.

Mohammad Khader started as a student of the oud. He now teaches it to children younger than himself, Reuters reported — one of several students the war has pushed early into teaching.

Not every student survived to make that move. Reuters named Yusuf Salman, a guitar student, among those killed.

## "An important tool for psychological relief"

> "Once music was entertainment and personal development. Now it's become an important tool for psychological relief."
> Ahmed Abu Amsha, conservatory teacher, to Reuters

Yara Abu Amsha, 15, still practises violin in the tents. The lesson plan has not changed as much as the walls around it have, teachers told Reuters: tabla, oud, guitar, flute and choral singing, the same instruments the conservatory taught before the war.

The recovery use of music reaches beyond the conservatory's own students. Fouad Khader has run music sessions for displaced people and, separately, for Gaza's field journalists through the Mayasem Association in Khan Younis, the Progressive reported in August.

A 21-year-old field reporter named Hassan told the outlet the sessions gave her "the feeling that I am not only a journalist documenting the suffering of others, but also a human being who needs rest."

## What rebuilding still needs

The conservatory's administration is seeking funds to rebuild its Gaza City building, the Institute for Palestine Studies reported, but almost no reconstruction has taken place in the Strip since October: Israeli restrictions on what may enter Gaza remain in force, and the ceasefire's stalled second phase has not opened the door for building materials at any scale.

Until that changes, the tents hold. Abu Amsha's choir still meets, Jahjouh's plastic flutes still play, and Daoud's students still come, in numbers no one has yet finished counting since the war moved the conservatory from a building in Tel al-Hawa to three tents by the sea.
