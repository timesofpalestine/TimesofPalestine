The most widely seen Palestinian story on television is a comedy about a family in Houston, and the man who manages the comedian who made it grew up in a family from Haifa.

Mustafa Abuelhija has spent more than twenty years in American comedy without ever standing on a stage. He manages Mo Amer, the Palestinian refugee turned stand-up whose Netflix series **Mo** put a Palestinian household — its grief, its olive oil, its immigration paperwork — into living rooms that had never knowingly encountered one.

That series won a Peabody Award and the Gotham Award for breakthrough series in 2022. It holds a 100 percent critics' rating on Rotten Tomatoes for its first season. Amer created and wrote it with Ramy Youssef, and its second and final season arrived in January 2025.

Abuelhija's name is not on the poster. It rarely is. His work happens in the part of the industry where careers are either built or quietly lost.

## From the Cellar floor

He started at the Comedy Cellar in Greenwich Village, the basement club on MacDougal Street where American stand-up is still measured, and rose to run it as general manager.

The Cellar is not a venue so much as a filter. Comedians drop in unannounced to test material in front of an audience sitting close enough to touch them. Managing the room means deciding who goes up, in what order, and in front of whom — which is another way of saying it means knowing, before anyone else does, who is about to be good.

It was there, around 2000, that he met Dave Chappelle.

## The room where the deal happened

By 2004 Abuelhija was Chappelle's personal manager, at the exact moment Chappelle became the most talked-about comedian alive. He helped negotiate the Comedy Central contract for the third and fourth seasons of *Chappelle's Show* — the deal reported at $50 million, one of the largest ever signed by a stand-up.

He also produced *Dave Chappelle's Block Party*, the Michel Gondry concert film that took Kanye West, Erykah Badu, Mos Def, Dead Prez and a reunited Fugees to a street corner in Bedford-Stuyvesant and turned an afternoon into one of the most affectionate documents of Black American music of its decade.

The partnership ended the following year, and in 2008 Abuelhija sued Chappelle over unpaid commissions from the period. Court reporting at the time recorded the claim and the dispute over what he was owed.

What the episode establishes, whatever its resolution, is the position he occupied: not an assistant to a famous man, but the person negotiating on the other side of a landmark contract.

## Building the room instead

In 2018 he co-founded Mint Comedy in New York with Omar Elrabie, a platform to stream live stand-up out of the clubs themselves — an attempt to take the Cellar's basement economy and give it a wider door.

Since November 2021 he has been an executive producer at Netflix.

And he manages Mo Amer, whose **Mo Amer: Wild World** he executive produced for the platform in 2025, alongside Mike Bertolina.

## Why this matters here

Palestinians are accustomed to being narrated. The camera arrives after the strike, stays for the funeral, and leaves. What has been scarcer is the ordinary register — a Palestinian family that argues about money, an asylum hearing that drags across years, a mother's cooking as a form of inheritance rather than a symbol.

*Mo* is that register, and it reached a global audience on the largest streaming service in the world. Amer wrote it and carried it. But a comedian does not arrive on Netflix alone. Somebody spends years learning which rooms matter, which contracts protect an artist and which quietly do not, and how a specific voice survives contact with an industry that prefers general ones.

Abuelhija learned that in a basement on MacDougal Street, applied it at the height of Chappelle's fame, and has spent the current decade applying it to a Palestinian story.

For a people whose narration has usually been done by others, having one of their own in the room where the deal is signed is not a footnote to the culture. It is part of how the culture gets made at all.
