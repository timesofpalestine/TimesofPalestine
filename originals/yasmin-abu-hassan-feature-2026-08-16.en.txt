Somewhere in Amman, most days of the week, a woman in a chef's coat looks into a phone camera, laughs at herself mid-sentence, and stirs a pot her grandmother would recognize. A million and a half people lean in to watch.

Yasmin Abu Hassan describes her work in five words on her Instagram page: serving happiness through my recipes. It is the rare bio that undersells.

## The engineer who chose the kitchen

Nothing about her path pointed here. She studied computer science at Amman's Princess Sumaya University for Technology, then trained in Hollywood's cinematic special-effects makeup, the Sayidaty magazine recounted in a profile — a résumé built for anywhere but a home kitchen.

Marriage changed the schedule that film work demanded, and the kitchen absorbed the hours. She began posting recipes to Instagram, and something unusual happened: she started to feel responsible for the strangers cooking alongside her. The recipes grew more precise, written so a beginner could not fail.

The Jordanian site Madar al-Saa traced the whole arc in one headline: from a pot of kousa b'laban to a cookbook.

## Sincerity as a cooking style

Her fame runs on a register the food business rarely manages: she is unpolished on purpose. Sky News Arabia's morning program profiled her as a case study in honest digital presence — a creator whose audience trusts her because what she shares looks like a real Tuesday, not a set.

The beloved recurring guest is her father, appearing beside her to stir a pan of potato-and-egg mufarakeh while she narrates. Those videos travel because every viewer has stood in that kitchen with their own father, or wishes they still could.

> Serving happiness through my recipes.
> Yasmin Abu Hassan's five-word manifesto, atop a page 1.5 million people follow

The audience became an institution. Her first cookbook — "My Dishes with Yasmin Abu Hassan: Recipes from the Heart" — arrived as twin editions, Arabic and English, 316 pages each, spanning the Arab table and the Western one.

The Jordanian outlet Jordan Zad reported from the launch, where she signed copies amid a crowd of family and longtime followers.

## One table, both banks of the river

Open her page on any given week and the menu reads like the Levant refusing to be partitioned: mansaf in its quick weeknight form, yalanji fatteh, kubbeh salad, stuffed potato boats under tahini, labneh on toast for the children's mornings.

This is the shared table of bilad al-Sham, and no border on any map runs through it. The dishes Amman's kitchens send up are the dishes of Nablus and Hebron and Jerusalem; the same rice steams on both banks of the Jordan.

For the enormous Palestinian share of her audience — in Jordan, across the West Bank, and scattered through the diaspora — her videos are not instruction so much as memory with the sound on.

That may be the quiet achievement under all the cheerfulness. In a decade when the region's news divides its people into jurisdictions, a chef in a home kitchen reminds a few million of them, one recipe at a time, that they were always one table.

The happiness she promised to serve, it turns out, was never only in the food.
